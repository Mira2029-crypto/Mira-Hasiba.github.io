Baja SAE - DAQ

Baja SAE

Overview

The Northwestern Baja electronics subteam was founded during the 2025–2026 academic year. As a relatively new team, we have many opportunities to improve the car’s data acquisition (DAQ) system for this year’s competition. One major change is that the work has been divided into two projects: Back DAQ and Front DAQ/HUD. I have been working on the Front DAQ/HUD.

DAQ System

This year, our goal is to embed the HUD screen in the Baja car’s steering wheel, making it easy for the driver to view while keeping it from obstructing their vision. We are also adding GPS and radio modules so the vehicle can be tracked and can transmit data over long distances to team members waiting in the pits.

Design and Implementation

This project is expected to be completed in May 2027.

Throughout the summer, I focused on selecting a radio, GPS module, shock sensors, and a screen for the Front DAQ. We are also using the same method as last year to measure wheel speed: Hall effect sensors determine the wheels’ revolutions per minute. In addition, the car will use the same power circuitry and a CAN system that is consistent across all of its PCBs this year.

GPS

A main goal of the DAQ is to provide live GPS tracking of the new vehicle, allowing the team to monitor it during a race and locate it if it breaks down out of sight. This summer, I evaluated GPS modules against four constraints:

  • Precision: Accurate enough to provide useful position tracking.
  • Connection: Reliable over long distances and in rough terrain.
  • Pin count: Uses as few ESP32 pins as possible, since GPIO is limited.
  • Size: Small enough to fit on the 68.5 mm × 83 mm steering wheel PCB.

After comparing several options, I chose the SparkFun GNSS L1/L5 Breakout - NEO-F10N, SMA. Three features set it apart from the other modules I considered:

  • Dual-band (L1/L5) reception: The receiver can combine signals from both bands to reduce multipath error and provide meter-level positional accuracy, even in urban areas or under tree cover.
  • Multi-constellation support: It tracks GPS, Galileo, and BeiDou, making more satellites available in challenging conditions.
  • Low pin usage: It communicates over UART and requires four connections total; power, ground, TX, and RX, only two of which use ESP32 GPIO pins.

The module also starts quickly, with a cold-start time of about 28 seconds and a hot-start time of about 2 seconds, thanks to its onboard RTC backup battery.

Radio

The other major addition to this year’s DAQ is a radio transceiver that sends data back to the team in real time. This lets us analyze the car’s data while it’s running and pinpoint what’s failing, so other subteams can fix problems based on data instead of guesswork.

I chose the Adafruit RFM95W LoRa Radio Transceiver at 915 MHz, the license-free band in the US. Adafruit rates it for over 2 km with line of sight and a simple wire antenna, and up to 20 km with directional antennas and tuning, which is more than we will need during our races.

Tradeoff: the module uses SPI, which is fast and well supported, but it takes several pins to use.

Shocks

This is the first year our team is implementing suspension sensing. My teammates and I researched how other Baja SAE teams approached it at past competitions, compared several options, and settled on linear potentiometers mounted to the shocks. They measure how far each shock compresses during a race, giving us real-time data on how the suspension handles jumps and rough terrain to verify that everything works as designed.

HUD

Alongside the DAQ, we’re adding a 2.43-inch OLED display that shows key data to the driver in real time, including fuel level from the Back DAQ and wheel speed, with room for more features later in the year. It mounts in the steering wheel, so it doesn’t block the driver’s view of the course.

Featured project

StepBuddy

January 2026–March 2026 | Niles Township for Special Education | Design, Thinking, and Communication

StepBuddy is a modified walking aid designed to help a local student with scoliosis, Down syndrome, and limited walking mobility move around school comfortably and safely.

Overview

StepBuddy was created to address the challenge of improving walking stability through a simple, accessible device that blends supportive hardware with user-friendly feedback. The goal was to create a practical solution that feels intuitive while still being technically capable and easy to prototype.

Primary User

The primary user is a 7th-grade middle school student with Down syndrome, limited mobility, hearing, speech, eyesight, cognitive abilities, and significant scoliosis who mainly relies on the use of a wheelchair, with occasional use of a walking aid. To use the walking aid, the user relies heavily on feelings of motivation, as well as something to lean on while using his walking aid. He has previously rejected using a traditional walking aid, but willingly uses his shopping cart in the school environment.

Primary Requirements
  • Must be Stable and Resist Tipping
  • Must be Maneuverable in School Hallways
  • Supports Natural Posture
  • Allow for Sensory Engagement and Motivation
  • Allow for Storage
  • Must be designed to look like his current shopping cart

Design Process

Taking in the requirements above, my team and I focused on a few major areas as we designed the StepBuddy: the handlebars, an LED matrix to stimulate the user, and various sensory details covering the StepBuddy. The frame of the StepBuddy was purchased and my team and I made the necessary modifications to raise its preexisting handlebars. However, after speaking with our project partner from Niles Township District for Special Education and observing the user, we realized the user may benefit from two additional ways to grip the cart.

In our design, we crafted two additional sets of handlebars for the users' convenience and comfort. For our first set, we built handlebars that would be parallel to the ground at a height that would help the user keep his arms at a 45-degree angle, which was recommended by his Physical Therapist. The second set consisted of handlebars that are perpendicular to the ground, as another spot the user can grab onto, giving him more of a choice in what he wants to do with the cart day by day.

The user responded well to these handlebars during our observations of the user interacting with our prototype. Another problem we wanted to address was that the user needed some sort of visual stimulation and interactive features in order to feel motivated to use the cart. We decided to implement an LED matrix that introduces a visual element with different colors, patterns, and designs to keep the user engaged while walking. The matrix is positioned at the user's eye level to discourage looking down and encourage the user to keep his head up and maintain better posture while walking. This is especially beneficial for his health in the long run, as the user suffers from scoliosis and experiences difficulty maintaining his head position naturally. We hoped that this visual stimulation would help him improve his posture over time.

Key Features

  • 3 handlebar types
  • Sensory stimulation on cart
  • LED matrix
  • Storage compartment

Results

In March of 2026, my team and I presented the StepBuddy at the Northwestern Engineering Design Expo. We presented to our project partner at Niles Township for Special Education and to other professors, students, and staff who attended the expo. Following this presentation, the StepBuddy was delivered to the user so that it may begin to help the user in their day-to-day life.

Featured project

PillPal

April 2026–June 2026 | Design, Thinking, and Communication

PillPal is a medication reminder and tracking device designed to help users manage daily prescriptions with confidence, clarity, and ease.

Overview

Many individuals struggle with medication adherence due to forgetfulness, inconsistent routines, or complex organization. Traditional organizers lack active reminders, secure access, support for injectable medications, and caregiver-friendly features. The objective of this project was to design and prototype PillPal. A lightweight, portable, and easy-to-refill smart pillbox that improves medication organization, accessibility, and adherence.

Primary User

The users of our design will be anyone who has a large number of medications they take daily, users with medical conditions that may result in memory loss, and anyone in general who needs a way to organize their pills and other medications that may be topical, injections, or any other forms of medications in one place.

Primary Requirements

As we conducted our research through various questionnaires and interviews, we decided to design a new type of medication housing system that will not only house pills but also syringes and other types of medication the users may need.

  • Solvability: In comparison to other projects we considered pursuing, we believed that this project would give us the experiences we hoped to grow in while being solvable within the time frame of this course.
  • Lack of Cost-Effective Existing Solutions: Through our market research, a majority of the solutions we found to address this problem were expensive, lacked certain key features, or had certain flaws that affected how certain users were able to interact with the product.
  • Locking Mechanism for Medications: The primary goal of this pill box is to have a system that will lock the user's medications until it's the designated time/day that it must be taken. This locking mechanism will be applied to all compartments added to the pill box.
  • Storage for Other Forms of Medications: Through our research, we found that a majority of pill boxes don't take into account medications that need to be administered topically or through syringes or that need to be refrigerated. As a result, one of our requirements for this project is to add a compartment that can house some of these medications.
  • OLED Screen to Display Medications: To help users keep track of their medications, our goal is to add a screen that displays a list of medications the user must take on a given day. This list will take into account both pills and other forms of medication that may need to be stored in other places.

Design Process

Northwestern University's Design, Thinking, and Communication (DTC) course tasked our team of first-year engineering students with identifying and resolving a specific user need that is currently overlooked or has an inadequate existing solution. To isolate this issue, our methodology integrated collaborative brainstorming, structured questionnaires, and direct observation of inefficiencies in daily life. Through these research methods, we noticed that a significant portion of the individuals who responded mentioned struggling with medication adherence due to forgetfulness.

PillPal is an automated pill dispenser divided into three compartments: one for medication, another for syringes and miscellaneous items, and the last for electronics that help the device perform all of its functions. Together, the compartments allow the box to lock, unlock, and display the medications users need to take at different times of day. This helps people managing multiple medications remember what they need to take, eliminating the frustration of missed doses and the potentially harmful effects of overdosing or underdosing.

I worked on designing the electrical system for PillPal, which consisted of one worm motor, one servo motor, four buttons, and one OLED screen. The worm motor rotates both the pill and syringe compartments of the box simultaneously, as this was found to be more efficient than rotating only the pill compartment. The worm motor is programmed to rotate 51 degrees once a day at 8:00 a.m. to unlock the next day's supply of pills. The servo motor locks the syringe compartment and unlocks it three times a day until the user presses a button indicating that they have finished; the servo then returns to its original position to lock the compartment again. One of the defining features of our automated pillbox is the OLED screen embedded in the lid. The screen reminds users which medications they need to take each day and is programmed to display the time, date, and a task list of daily medications. Users can use the built-in buttons to scroll through the list and mark off the medications they have taken throughout the day.

Schematics for Electronics System

Key Features

  • Rotating Housing System: Features a three-compartment internal housing system divided into morning, afternoon, and evening sections.
  • Structural Stability: The internal housing rotates beneath a fixed upper structure, minimizing wiring complications and increasing stability.
  • Electronic Reminders: Integrates a programmable microcontroller and display interface to actively notify users when it is time to take their medication.
  • Various Storage Compartments: The first layer of PillPal houses the user's pills, allowing them to store as many as they need within compartments of different sizes. This makes the device more accessible to users with a large number of medications. The second layer has two compartments: the first locks to store medications the user intends to use daily, such as syringes and topical medications. The other compartment does not lock, allowing users to store lifesaving medications or medications they need frequently without worrying about them being locked away. This design houses all forms of medication in one place.

Results

We had the opportunity to present our work to our classmates and a panel of professors who provided feedback on our design. Although the prototype was developed under practical constraints, including budget, time, manufacturing limitations, and available resources, it demonstrates a meaningful step toward improving medication organization and independence. Overall, PillPal demonstrates how user-centered design and engineering decisions can create a more inclusive and practical approach to medication management.
